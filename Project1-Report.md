# Report on the Geographic Health Equity in Seattle

Dear Seattle Non-Profit,
Health equity is a very important part of determining whether the wellbeing of a population
of people is being taken care of. Studying maps of health equity is beneficial for pointing
out areas with disparities in health outcomes and can also reveal if there are any
inequalities with access to health services. Mapping these health outcomes as an index is
especially useful when it comes to visually showing the specific areas struggling with
health outcomes and can be used by policy makers to have places to focus on for creating
solutions. The index used these health outcomes in the figures below:
- 1 Percentage of adults with no leisure-time physical activity
- 3 Percentage of adults with obesity
- 6 Life expectancy at birth
- 7 Percentage of adults with disability
- 8 Percentage of insured civilian noninstitutionalized population

  
These five variables were calculated using US Census Bureau data and were mapped using
a quantile classification. Each of the variables mapped were classified into 5 categories or
classes ranging from “Very High” to “Very Low”.

In figure 1, the variable classified is the
percentage of adults who engage in physical activities in their leisure-time. It’s shown that
majority of Southern Seattle such as the Rainer Valley and Beacon Hill areas that have very
high levels of adults not doing much physical activity, and with the Northen most part of
Seattle as-well. It seems to mainly be the main city part of Seattle’s population that
engages in physical activities the most which leads more healthier lifestyles. Figure 2 is
showing a measure of the percentage of adults classified as obese. We can see that
census tracts in the Southern neighborhoods of Seattle have higher levels of adult obesity
which correlates with access to healthy food and socioeconomic status of the populations
there. The Western parts such as Georgetown have a large cluster of tracts with higher
levels of adult obesity, whereas tracts near the Fremont and University District have much
less obesity levels. 

Figure 3 shows the life expectancy at birth based on health and
mortality data. The census tracts with low to very low life expectancies can show that
persisting health inequalities can abect people from the very start. Neighborhoods in
Georgetown have a large clustering of tracts with very low life expectancies and would
mean a systemic issue with health outcomes. If a certain population is already at a
disadvantage with their health outcomes, this could be passed on to the future children
being born as their environment and living lifestyle has a huge ebect on how long they may
live. 

Figure 4 maps out the percentage of adults living with a disability, and there seems to
be groupings of census tracks where the higher leveled tracts are close together such as in
the Fremont neighborhood. This figure can be used as a good starting point for where to
add or improve health facilities or infrastructure for disabled adults. Figure 5 shows the last
variable of the percentage of insured adults, and again it can be shown that Southern
census tracts of Seattle in the Beacon Hill/Georgetown neighborhoods have fewer insured
people. Compared to the couple groupings of North and Northeastern census tracts
around the Olympic Hills being well insured, there is an obvious gap in insurance coverage
between populations. Insurance coverage means better access to healthcare, while lower
coverage indicates gaps in accessible healthcare.

Figure 7 is the composite map of the health equity index of Seattle as a whole. Very high
inequity is concentrated in the Southern census tracts including areas in Rainer Valley,
Georgetown, Greenwood, and Lake City. Areas like Ballard or Magnolia rank very low for
inequity. The contrast in these areas show there are systemic inequalities to the access to
resources that help improve the overall health of people. The lowest equity census tracts
were tracts 145, 125, 43, 23, and 22. On the other hand, the tracts with highest equity were
tracts 167, 9, 161, 76, 11. These patterns all show there seems to be systemic barriers in
underserved areas such as poor infrastructure, poor healthcare access, and
socioeconomic obstacles.

In conclusion, the geographic landscape of health equity in Seattle shows there are clear
disparities when it comes to the well-being of certain populations. There is a trend with
Southern neighborhoods experiencing significantly more inequities compared to Northen
Seattle. Policy makers should make it a priority to address the gaps causing this, and to
focus on expanding healthcare access and improving infrastructure in the right places to
address the people who need it most. The figures mentioned in this report can be a helpful
tool in guiding eborts and resources to where they are going to be most impactful.
